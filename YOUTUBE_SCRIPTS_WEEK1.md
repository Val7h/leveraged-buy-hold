**[0:45–2:30] SOLUTION (105 seconds)**

**[Voiceover]**
"That's leverage. And it's not magic. It's not risky. It's just... math.

Leverage means using borrowed money to increase your investment position size.

Here's how it works: You have $100k. You borrow $50k from your broker. Now you have $150k to invest. That's 1.5x leverage.

When the market goes up 10%, your $150k investment gains $15,000. Minus interest you paid on the $50k borrowed (let's say $2,000), you net $13,000 profit. Way better than $10k.

BUT—and this is critical—if the market goes DOWN 10%, your $150k loses $15,000. Suddenly that $50k you borrowed is a bigger problem.

This is why most people don't use leverage. They're scared of losing control.

The good news? There's a formula—a 100-year-old math formula—that tells you EXACTLY how much leverage is safe for your portfolio."

**[2:30–4:00] THE FORMULA (90 seconds)**

**[Voiceover]**
"It's called the Kelly Criterion. Named after a mathematician at Bell Labs.

The formula looks like this: f equals b times p minus q, divided by b.

Okay, that's scary. Let me translate:

f = how much leverage is safe
b = how much you could make
p = how often you win
q = how often you lose

For the S&P 500, here's the math:
- You win about 75% of the time (positive returns)
- You lose about 25% of the time
- Average win is +10%
- Average loss is –8%

Plug that into Kelly, and you get: 1.5x leverage is mathematically optimal.

That means: You can safely borrow 50% extra without getting liquidated, even in a crash.

With 1.5x leverage on the S&P 500 since 2006, you would have earned 12.4% per year instead of 10.2%.

Over 20 years, that's the difference between $673k and $850k on a $100k investment.

That's $177,000 extra. Just by sizing leverage correctly."

- Then chart: "20-year backtest"

**[1:00–3:00] THE SCORING FORMULA (120 seconds)**

"Our score has two parts: Quality and Opportunity.

Quality is 60% of the score. It answers: 'Is this a good company?'

Quality measures:
- Dividend yield (higher = better for buy-and-hold)
- Beta (volatility; lower = safer)
- Sharpe ratio (risk-adjusted returns; higher = better)
- Profitability (margins, growth)
- Debt level (less = safer)

So a dividend-paying utility with low volatility gets a HIGH quality score.

A tech growth stock with no dividend and high volatility gets a LOWER quality score.

Opportunity is 40% of the score. It answers: 'Is NOW a good time to buy this stock?'

Opportunity measures:
- RSI (Relative Strength Index): Is it oversold (<30) or overbought (>70)?
- Price momentum: Is it trending up or down?
- Valuation: Is it cheap or expensive compared to history?

So if dividend stocks are normally boring, but today the market crashed and VTI is RSI 25 (oversold), Opportunity spikes.

Final Score = (0.6 × Quality) + (0.4 × Opportunity)

Range: 0 to 100.

>70 = Strong buy signal
30–70 = Neutral
<30 = Avoid or reduce"

**[4:00–5:30] CAVEATS & HONESTY (90 seconds)**

"Now, let's be honest about what this backtest DOESN'T prove.

1. Correlation breakdowns: In March 2020 and March 2008, all assets sold off together. Diversification didn't help. Our system doesn't predict that.

5. Slippage: Real execution is messier than this backtest. You might buy at 1.6x leverage but get filled at 1.55x.

So what does this backtest actually prove?

It proves: IF market conditions in 2026–2046 resemble 2006–2026, AND IF you size leverage using Kelly Criterion, AND IF you rebalance regularly, then leverage does improve returns materially.

3. Crisis stress test: Take 2008 dynamics and apply them to today's portfolio. Same 65% max drawdown? Yes. Liquidated? No. So the model is robust.

Not perfect. But more trustworthy than most backtests."
